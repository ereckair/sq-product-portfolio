01What is Ashley Director

Ashley Director is a quality-operations copilot for the whole team — hosted on xwork, reachable from any device, no install required. Every morning it reads the overnight flow of rejection emails, consumer complaints and test results, decides what actually needs a human decision today, and puts a short, ranked, evidence-backed queue in front of the reader — what to act on, who to call, and why, in one glance.

It is deliberately not a dashboard. Dashboards show everything and settle nothing. Ashley Director is built around a different promise: by the time the reader opens the app, the triage is already done. The day's decisions are on top; everything else waits where it can be found.

One-line summary: it doesn't just show data — it tells the team what to do today, ranked, with evidence attached.

02Who it is for

The product was proposed by Gab and built around the Quality Director's working day: morning triage, supplier calls, factory visits, and the constant question of "is this number fresh enough to defend?". Every screen is optimized for that first hour.

But it is not designed as a one-person tool. The same queue, evidence trail and briefing packs are exactly what a QA lead, a plant quality team, or whoever covers during an absence needs to stay aligned with the Director's view. As the platform matures, today's single view is expected to scale into per-role experiences on the same data foundation — see the Roadmap.

Corporate ITEvaluating the rebuild path onto governed infrastructure (see Roadmap).

04How the data gets there

The app is thin by design; the work happens in the pipeline behind it. Today that pipeline runs on a prototype backend that has processed 400+ real rejection threads. The corporate rebuild (next on the roadmap) replaces the plumbing, not the product.

SourcesRejection mailbox, consumer complaints, test results, vendor pricing & rosters
PipelineExtraction, classification, entity resolution (RP → item → vendor), freshness checks
Decision engineThresholds, pattern detection, 7-cap triage, confidence scoring
Ashley DirectorFive tabs, one queue, full evidence drill-down

Two properties matter more than the plumbing diagram:

  • Entity resolution is the hard part. An email mentions an RP; the queue needs the factory, the QA owner, the vendor contacts and the 30-day history. The pipeline maintains those mappings (RP → item → vendor, with demand-split aware vendor attribution) so the app never asks the reader to join tables in their head.
  • Freshness is a first-class feature. Every mapping source has an age. If a source is older than 30 days, the app raises a banner on the screens that depend on it — see Section 09.

05Queue — the daily decision list

The Queue is the product's front door. It answers one question: what deserves a decision today? — and, just as deliberately, what does not.

5.1The cap is the feature

Today holds at most seven items. Anything not ready for a decision waits in Watching until it earns attention; aged items move to Archive with a one-line closure reason. A queue of 40 is a queue of none — the cap mirrors real triage capacity, it is not a technical limit.

5.2Every entry explains itself

5.3Nothing is silently lost

Archive entries keep their closure reason ("resolved after containment audit", "carrier claim closed") — visible, reversible, never a black hole. If a closed pattern re-escalates, it re-enters the Queue as a new trigger, not as a zombie.

Business payoff: the team's first hour goes to deciding, not triaging.

06Vendors — the supplier directory

The Vendors tab turns "this keeps happening" from a feeling into a fact. It covers 150 suppliers with contacts, performance grades and repeat-offender signals.

6.1Contacts resolved automatically

Each vendor card carries the vendor number, office, QA team assignment, and the two people who matter on a call: the Vendor Coordinator (VC) and Corporate Intelligence (CI) contact. The lookup is done by the pipeline — no spreadsheets.

6.2One tap drafts the email

"Email QA Team about this RP" pre-fills a message with the case context attached — the reader confirms and sends. The grade (A–C, from peer-ranked rejections) and the defect mix are on the same screen, so the conversation starts from facts.

Business payoff: supplier calls start at the facts, not at the phone book.

07Briefings — the factory visit pack

Briefings prepare a 30-minute factory review from one page. Each pack answers three questions before the reader walks in: how is this factory rated, what should I prioritize, and what does it cost?

7.1A rating with its reasons

"High attention" is never a bare label — every rating drills down to why this rating: the audit KPIs behind it (AQL 96.67% passing, finish 88.89%, packing 8.47% failed units for the factory shown).

7.2Walk-in priorities, numbered

The pack lists which containment to observe first and what proof to demand — photos, re-audit, approved-standard comparison — each priority linked to its evidence.

7.3Money on the same screen

Monthly Repair & Allowance (R&A) spend — $4,519 in July, $2,849 under June for the example factory — sits next to the rejection rates by item that drive it (e.g. D743-02 at 15%). Quality and cost in the same conversation, whoever leads the review.

08QI — patterns across streams

One complaint is an incident; a pattern is a signal. The QI (Quality Import) tab watches every stream — 1,240 parts-rejection records, 276 fabric tests — not just the loudest email.

  • Rising patterns surface automatically — clusters that cross their threshold appear with the direction and magnitude of the move ("Up 4 vs prior 30 days").
  • Repeat offenders get named — the Vendors tab ranks factories by recurring defects, so the pattern becomes a supplier conversation.
  • Every number drills down to its source record — the original email, the test result, the complaint. No number without provenance.
Same case, three lenses — Queue for action, QI for patterns, Vendors for accountability. That's how a trend becomes a conversation with the supplier.

11Product principles

Four rules explain most of the design decisions in this guide:

1 · Decide, don't display

The app's job is to reduce the day to decisions that matter. A queue of 7 beats a dashboard of 400.

2 · Evidence is not optional

Every number, rating and flag carries its provenance. If it can't show its work, it doesn't ship.

3 · Honesty over green

"Partly ready" is an acceptable state; a stale-source banner is a feature. Trust is earned by admitting uncertainty.

4 · One shared view

Built around the Director's day first, but the same queue and evidence serve the whole quality operation — no private truth.

12Roadmap

From working prototype to corporate platform, in three deliberate steps:

Now

Working prototype

Web app on xwork, in daily use; the data pipeline has processed 400+ real rejection threads end to end.

Next

Corporate rebuild

IT rebuilds the backend on corporate infrastructure; the app switches to live enterprise data — same UX, governed sources.

Later

Quality Data Hub

All quality analytics fed from one internal data hub — vendor mappings, rosters and KPIs always current, retire-proof.

The app is the product — and it is device-agnostic. The prototype backend was scaffolding — the rebuild replaces it, the app doesn't change. Hosted on xwork, it opens on whatever device the reader has — phone, tablet or laptop — with no install. And as the platform matures, it won't stay a one-desk tool: the same queue, evidence and briefings can serve QA leads and plant teams, while today's single view scales into per-role experiences.

Sequencing note: the Quality Data Hub also de-risks the planned EDW retirement — it becomes the single analytical source for vendor mappings, rosters and KPIs.

13Making it enterprise-grade

The prototype proved the product works. Enterprise-grade is a different bar — not "it works" but "the company can trust it, operate it, and live without the person who built it". This section records honestly how we are getting there and where the hard parts are.

13.2The eight hard problems

Ranked by how much they hurt. The first two are the reason the rebuild takes real engineering, not just a migration.

1Entity resolution at the source

An email says "wrong part arrived". The queue needs: which RP, which item, which vendor at that time, which QA team owns it. The quality system keeps these relations with demand-split semantics — an item can map to several vendors, historical vendors stay attached at 0% split, and "first seen" ordering is not the same as majority ordering. Getting this wrong means pointing S1 complaints at the wrong factory. This was the single biggest data problem in the whole rebuild, and it was solved by going to the source system's own records and confirming semantics with the data owners — not by inferring from exports.

2The source system was never meant to be read

The authoritative tables belong to a vendor system that is not ours to modify. The enterprise path avoids changing it: read-only access where granted, the governed pipeline as the single extraction point, and — as the durable answer — the Quality Data Hub, so no product team ever depends on that system's internals again. This also hedges the planned retirement of the old warehouse (EDW), whose vendor-number formats don't even line up with the source of truth.

3Surviving the founder problem

A prototype maintained by its author is always one vacation away from darkness. Enterprise-grade means the Mac dispatcher heartbeat on the health screen becomes an actual operations commitment: monitored canaries, documented runbooks, and an IT team that can rebuild the pipeline from documentation alone. The rebuild is as much an organizational handover as a technical one.

4Trust has to be provable

In the prototype, "the numbers are fresh" was a promise. At enterprise scale it must be auditable: every feed has an age, every check on the health screen has a rule behind it, and the 30-day staleness banner becomes a contractual guarantee rather than a product courtesy. "Partly ready" must survive corporate scrutiny — an honest yellow beats a fake green.

5Scope discipline under new voices

Once a prototype works, every stakeholder sees their use case in it. The enterprise risk is not failure to extend — it is extending before the foundation is governed. The roadmap sequence (rebuild first, hub second, per-role experiences after) exists to keep the single shared view honest before it multiplies.

6Making implicit knowledge explicit

The prototype's hardest asset is not in the code. The demand-split rules, the match thresholds, the reasons a mapping is trusted or doubted — these live in the author's head and in months of one-off email threads. Enterprise-grade means writing them down where the system can enforce them: validation rules and data contracts that the pipeline checks mechanically, so the system raises an alarm when the data is "wrong" instead of relying on someone noticing. A rule that exists only as tribal memory is a rule the company cannot keep.

7The first exception sets the culture

Governance is easy until the first urgent case arrives. The moment a senior user asks to bypass the governed pipeline "just this once", the real test begins: if the exception is granted quietly, a shadow copy of the data starts growing somewhere and the single shared view quietly loses its authority. The answer is not rigidity — it is a fast, documented exception path, so the first "no" comes with a legitimate route instead of a workaround.

8Landing the transition

Enterprise-grade is also about the switch itself. During the parallel-run period the governed pipeline and the prototype will not agree perfectly — the split-semantics work means they should diverge on the messy cases. So "cutover ready" cannot mean "numbers match yesterday's"; it means agreed acceptance criteria: a defined adjudication process for each class of divergence, replay-safe feeds (re-running a day must not double-count it), source-tolerance to schema drift, and observability that can answer "why did this number change?" — lineage plus a day-over-day diff, not just a green dashboard.

The pattern across all eight: the hard parts were never the app itself. They were semantics, ownership and trust — making sure the right vendor is attached to the right case, that someone runs it after the author leaves, and that the company can defend every number it shows.

13.3Three more, from the author

Beyond the engineering problems above, the product's author names three harder ones — they explain why the enterprise path looks the way it does.

First structured data in the decision loop

This is the first time structured data has been pushed directly into a daily decision chain at Ashley. That makes the app more than a product — it is a rehearsal for the SOR itself: the entity semantics recovered during the rebuild (vendor/RP mappings, demand splits) are exactly what the system of record should eventually consume back. The bar this sets is higher than "an app that works" — the semantics must be clean enough to be promoted into the SOR, not just read from it.

The stack was personal

Like most vibe-coding projects, large parts of the prototype are fitted to the author's own equipment — a Mac Studio running the dispatch pipeline, local paths, personal automation habits. None of that is a flaw; it is how the prototype got built in weeks. But it means enterprise-grade is not a deployment, it is a rebuild on an enterprise technology stack — which is exactly why the roadmap starts there rather than with new features.

Iteration has to flow back

Users will keep experimenting in their own vibe-coding projects — that is healthy and should not be stopped. The hard question is how their improvements get extracted and merged back into the enterprise project instead of forking into private copies. The plan: once the enterprise version ships, open its code to the users and let them iterate on that baseline. The official repository becomes the shared trunk — the same answer to the shadow-copy risk in problem 7, but as an open door rather than a locked one.

15FAQ

Is the data in the screenshots real?

Yes. Every screenshot in this guide and in the overview deck is from the live app running on production data — August 2026: 419 rejection emails, 321 threads processed end-to-end, 1,240 parts-rejection records, 276 fabric tests, 150 suppliers.

Why is the queue capped at 7?

On purpose. The cap mirrors real triage capacity — a queue of 40 is a queue of none. Items that don't make today's list wait in Watching and earn their way in; nothing is lost (Archive keeps closure reasons), but nothing clutters the day either.

What happens if a data source goes stale?

The app warns before it misleads: any mapping source older than 30 days raises a banner on every screen that depends on it. The health screen also shows which checks are pending ("Partly ready" is an allowed state).

Does the corporate rebuild change the app?

No. The rebuild replaces the prototype backend with governed corporate infrastructure and live enterprise data. The UX, the rules and the five tabs stay the same — that is the point of "the app doesn't change".

Who uses it besides the Quality Director?

Today the product is optimized around the Director's working day, but it is not a one-person tool: the same queue, evidence trail and briefing packs keep QA leads and the wider quality team aligned with the same view. Per-role experiences are on the roadmap as the platform matures.

How do I get access or ask for a change?

Access requests and change proposals go through the Quality Operations team. The health screen ("More" tab) is the first place to check if something looks off — it says exactly what the pipeline has verified and what is still pending.
